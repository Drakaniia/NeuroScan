# 2 REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE

This chapter establishes what is already known about brain tumor classification from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and what comparable systems have been built to perform it. Section 2.1 reviews the related literature, Section 2.2 describes and compares the related systems, Section 2.3 presents the conceptual framework of the study, and Section 2.4 defines the terms used in it.

## 2.1 Related Literature

Deep learning has been applied to brain tumor detection and classification from MRI with consistently promising results across the studies covered by a systematic review of 60 articles published between 2020 and January 2024 ([Bouhafra & El Bahi, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12092918/)), and the review below is therefore organized by topic rather than in order of publication. It begins with the clinical problem and the imaging modality, proceeds through the detection, classification, and segmentation tasks that deep learning is asked to perform on brain MRI, and then examines the methodological choices that shape what a reported result means — transfer learning and data handling, lightweight architectures, explainability, and evaluation — before closing with how such tools are used in clinical decision support and where their inference can be performed. Section 2.1.11 states what this body of work jointly supports and which gaps the present study takes up.

### 2.1.1 Brain tumors and the role of magnetic resonance imaging

A brain tumor is characterized by the abnormal, uncontrolled multiplication of cells within the brain or its coverings, and its symptoms vary with the affected region, ranging from headaches and memory loss to hemiparesis and aphasia ([Bouhafra & El Bahi, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12092918/)). The World Health Organization's 2021 classification of central nervous system tumors distinguishes more than 200 tumor types on the basis of location and histopathological features, which makes differentiation difficult in the case of small lesions ([Bouhafra & El Bahi, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12092918/)). This complexity is one reason early and accurate detection is important: brain tumors disrupt normal brain function as they grow, and precise classification is required before treatment can be planned ([Anand et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12868662/)).

Among the imaging modalities used to evaluate brain tumors, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the most effective: compared with computed tomography, MRI provides superior contrast of brain anatomical regions and is therefore the preferred method for identifying brain tumor malignancy ([Bouhafra & El Bahi, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12092918/)). MRI is likewise described as the most common imaging technique for detecting and diagnosing brain tumors, on account of its greater contrast in soft tissues ([Aksoy et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12388561/)), and it is favored over the other imaging techniques used to confirm brain tumors because it provides superior contrast resolution and anatomical detail without ionizing radiation ([Missaoui et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12074157/)). The interpretation of these images, however, is time-consuming, requires specialized expertise, and is subject to inter-observer variability, which is why manual reading of large MRI volumes remains error-prone ([Kantu et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13103188/); [Aksoy et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12388561/)). Conventional MRI does not always settle the question it is used to answer, since a wide range of non-neoplastic brain lesions can mimic tumors on MRI and differentiating neoplastic from non-neoplastic lesions, as well as high-grade from low-grade tumors, can be difficult and can lead to unnecessary biopsies; in a single-centre retrospective study of 37 patients in a tertiary hospital in the Philippines, spectroscopy and perfusion imaging were reported to complement conventional MRI in that differentiation ([Co et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12033976/)). The growing demand for MRI examinations together with a limited number of radiologists has consequently drawn attention to computer-aided diagnosis, which is credited with reducing clinician workload and minimizing the risk of human error ([Bouhafra & El Bahi, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12092918/)).

The pressure on imaging services is markedly greater in low- and middle-income countries, where equipment and workforce shortages are documented: fewer than one computed tomography scanner per million inhabitants compared with almost 40 per million in high-income countries, a gap that is wider still for MRI, and 1.9 versus 97.9 radiologists per million inhabitants ([Frija et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8318869/)). In that setting, specialist radiological expertise may simply not be available, which is the diagnostic gap that lightweight, locally deployable screening tools are proposed to address ([Abdusalomov et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11759171/)).

### 2.1.2 Detection, classification, and segmentation from brain MRI

Research that applies deep learning to brain MRI is organized around distinct tasks whose outputs are not interchangeable, and the review literature treats them separately, addressing the challenges of brain tumor analysis across detection, segmentation, classification, and survival prediction ([Missaoui et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12074157/)). Detection asks whether a tumor is present and where it is, and a detector therefore reports a localization with a confidence score rather than a class label: a lightweight RetinaNet variant designed for medical edge devices reported an average precision of 32.1 on volumetric BraTS cases, decomposing into 14.3 for small tumors and 49.7 for large ones, with confidence scores above 81% ([Abdusalomov et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11759171/)). Classification instead assigns an image to one tumor category among several, which is why classification studies report accuracy across glioma, meningioma, pituitary, and no-tumor classes ([Anand et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12868662/)). Segmentation identifies the spatial extent of the tumor and is scored by overlap with a reference delineation, which is why a platform that performs classification and volumetric segmentation together reported Dice coefficients of approximately 68% to 70% for segmentation while its classifier reached 98.09% — two figures that describe different tasks and are not comparable to each other ([Aksoy et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12388561/)).

NeuroScan addresses one narrow form of classification: a single two-dimensional slice is labelled tumor present or no tumor. That scope is the study's own design choice rather than a claim drawn from the literature. The reviewed detection and segmentation systems are evaluated on volumetric input, with localization or delineation as the output ([Abdusalomov et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11759171/); [Aksoy et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12388561/)), whereas a screening decision requires one label per image, and a per-image verdict, confidence score, and activation heatmap are what this study sets out to deliver.

### 2.1.3 Deep learning for brain tumor classification

A systematic review of 60 studies published between 2020 and January 2024 found that deep learning methods — including transfer learning, autoencoders, transformers, attention mechanisms, and generative adversarial networks — have been applied to brain tumor detection and classification from MRI with consistently promising results ([Bouhafra & El Bahi, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12092918/)). Individual studies report high classification accuracies on public datasets: a proposed four-block convolutional neural network reached an overall accuracy of 98.5% across glioma, meningioma, pituitary, and no-tumor categories, exceeding the transfer learning models it was compared against, although its per-class accuracy ranged from 0.86 for glioma to 0.98 for the no-tumor class ([Anand et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12868662/)), while a web-deployed platform using a fine-tuned MobileNetV2 recorded 98.09% classification accuracy on the same four categories ([Aksoy et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12388561/)).

A review of 83 CNN-based studies of brain tumor classification puts those figures in context: reported accuracies range from 91.63% to 100% for differentiating meningiomas, gliomas, and pituitary tumors, and the authors conclude that no specific methodological choice clearly outperforms the alternatives, particularly because validation methods, performance metrics, and training data are reported inconsistently across the literature ([Xie et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9406354/)). An accuracy figure therefore describes a study's own dataset and protocol as much as the model it reports, which is the point taken up in §2.1.8.

### 2.1.4 Transfer Learning and Data Challenges in Brain MRI Classification

Transfer learning is widely used in this domain because a pre-trained network can produce better accuracy even when the number of training samples is small, which is typical of medical imaging datasets ([Rasa et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11459499/)). In a comparison of six fine-tuned architectures, augmentation was applied to enlarge the image pool and reduce overfitting, and the models were additionally validated with cross-validation ([Rasa et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11459499/)). A separate study that fine-tuned AlexNet, MobileNetV2, and GoogleNet on 4,517 MRI scans reported that it addressed class imbalance through data augmentation, and that it deliberately included a lightweight architecture to keep deployment practical; its best model reached 99.2% accuracy ([Khan et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12149374/)). Augmentation for class imbalance is the stated remedy in a web-deployed framework as well, where fusing three fine-tuned networks was preceded by augmentation intended to ensure a well-distributed training set ([Ahmmed et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12876050/)).

Reported accuracy moves with the data and the validation protocol, not with the architecture alone. The same six-architecture framework reached 100% testing accuracy on its smaller datasets without cross-validation, 99.96% with cross-validation, and 96.34% to 98.20% when larger datasets were used ([Rasa et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11459499/)). Conversely, three custom convolutional networks whose hyperparameters were tuned by grid search, and which the authors describe as trained on large and publicly available clinical datasets, reported 99.33% for detection, 92.66% for five-class classification, and 98.14% for three-grade classification without using transfer learning at all ([Irmak, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8061452/)). Neither result overturns the other; together they indicate that dataset size, class distribution, and the validation protocol are as decisive as the choice of backbone ([Rasa et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11459499/); [Irmak, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8061452/)).

Data handling is the recurring practical difficulty behind those numbers. A review of lightweight classification models observes that earlier works have not always ensured careful data handling and have introduced data leakage through augmented test sets or unstandardized dataset splits ([Kantu et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13103188/)). Because reported figures depend on exactly those choices, evaluation design — partitioning, held-out testing, and the avoidance of leakage — is treated as a topic in its own right in §2.1.8.

### 2.1.5 Lightweight architectures and deployment feasibility

Deep architectures such as VGG-16, ResNet-50, and InceptionV3 are computationally intensive, which makes them less practical for deployment in low-resource or real-time clinical environments ([Kantu et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13103188/)). Lightweight models have therefore been evaluated as substitutes: MobileNetV3 achieved 99.31% test accuracy with 3.47 million parameters on a 7,023-image dataset, outperforming heavier baseline architectures at a fraction of the parameter count ([Kantu et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13103188/)). In an explicitly deployment-oriented study, a RetinaNet variant whose ResNet backbone was replaced with MobileNet and depthwise separable convolutions was designed for medical edge devices; on the BraTS dataset it reached an average precision of 32.1, with 14.3 for small tumors and 49.7 for large tumors, and the authors argue that this lightweight design makes accurate localization feasible on low-power hardware in underserved regions ([Abdusalomov et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11759171/)). The two results are not directly comparable, since one measures four-class classification accuracy on static images while the other measures object-detection precision on volumetric data — a reminder that the deployment benefit of a light architecture varies with the task it is asked to perform.

### 2.1.6 Explainability of CNN-based classification

High predictive performance is not the only consideration when a model is intended for medical use. The complexity of deep neural networks makes their decisions difficult to interpret, and models may attend to non-discriminative or irrelevant features such as normal soft tissues instead of tumor regions, which undermines the reliability of a classification ([Iftikhar et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12044100/)). Lack of transparency and interpretability is likewise identified as a critical barrier to the adoption of AI in healthcare, because physicians are reluctant to rely on recommendations from systems they do not fully understand ([Abbas et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12427955/)).

Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques are used to address this problem, and the family most directly connected to this study is class activation mapping. Class activation mapping generates a heatmap highlighting the regions of an input image that contribute most to a specific class prediction ([Nazir et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11497403/)). Gradient-weighted variants of the same idea are widely reported alongside classification models: a ResNet50 model paired with class activation mapping reached 98.52% testing accuracy, with precision and recall each above 98%, while providing visual explanations of the areas the network attended to ([Mohamed Musthafa et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11088067/)), and a MobileNetV3 and SqueezeNet pair used class activation mapping to confirm that its predictions followed tumor-relevant regions rather than background tissue ([Kantu et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13103188/)).

Other explanation families appear alongside these in the literature and are used differently. Studies that combine visualizations with SHAP and LIME report that the resulting explanations clarify which image regions drive the output ([Iftikhar et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12044100/); [Nazir et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11497403/)), a customized CNN has been paired with all three families together ([Nazir et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11497403/)), and a platform that extended explainability to volumetric output used saliency maps for its two-dimensional predictions and Gaussian overlays for its three-dimensional visualizations ([Aksoy et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12388561/)). Those techniques are presented as distinct from class activation mapping in the sources above ([Iftikhar et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12044100/); [Aksoy et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12388561/)), and they are not the technique this study implements: NeuroScan produces a class activation map from the exported weights of its classification head, so the mapping family described above is the primary evidence here and the remaining techniques are context.

The evaluation of these explanations, however, remains an acknowledged weakness of the field. A meta-analysis of 62 peer-reviewed studies found that model-agnostic visualization techniques dominate in imaging tasks, yet identified gaps in user-friendly evaluation, methodological transparency, and ethics — including an absence of research that evaluated explanation fidelity, clinician trust, or usability in real-world settings — and recommended longitudinal clinical validation and participatory system design ([Abbas et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12427955/)). This indicates that producing a heatmap is not the same as demonstrating that it is useful to the person who must act on it.

### 2.1.7 Evaluation metrics for medical image classification

A screening decision is only as trustworthy as the way it is measured. For a binary classifier, the common metrics are all computed from four quantities — true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives — and model development is organized into training, validation, and test phases in which the final figures are calculated on a test set held aside once training and tuning are complete ([Hicks et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8993826/)). Accuracy is the ratio of correctly classified samples to the total number of samples, and it is the measure most often quoted; it is also the one that misleads most readily, because its value depends on how the classes are distributed, so a model can appear accurate on an imbalanced dataset while performing poorly on the class of interest ([Hicks et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8993826/)). Recall, or sensitivity, is the rate of positive samples that are correctly classified, which clinically is the proportion of true positive results among all patients who have the condition, while specificity is the corresponding rate for the negative class — the percentage of true negatives among all subjects who do not have the condition — and the two move in opposite directions as the decision threshold changes ([Shreffler & Huecker, 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK557491/)). Precision is the ratio of correctly classified samples to all samples assigned to that class, and its positive-class form is the positive predictive value ([Hicks et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8993826/)). The F1 score combines precision and recall into a single harmonic mean, which penalizes extreme values of either measure and is not symmetric between the two classes ([Hicks et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8993826/)). Because no single metric captures every desirable property, several are normally reported together ([Hicks et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8993826/)). This study reports accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, F1, ROC-AUC, and the confusion matrix from which the remaining measures are derived, with each figure reported overall and per source dataset.

Two further measures matter when a classifier returns a probability rather than a fixed label, as this one does. Receiver operating characteristic analysis applies to tests that yield continuous outputs, and the area under the curve summarizes the test's ability to distinguish diseased from non-diseased individuals across all cutoff values, ranging from 0.5 for no better than chance to 1.0 for perfect discrimination, generally read as clinically useful above 0.80, and always interpreted together with its confidence interval ([Çorbacıoğlu & Aksel, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10664195/)). The Youden index identifies the cutoff value that maximizes sensitivity and specificity together, which is what makes it an appropriate rule for selecting an operating threshold ([Çorbacıoğlu & Aksel, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10664195/)). This study follows that rule with a deliberate emphasis: the threshold is chosen on the validation set to maximize the index subject to a sensitivity of at least 0.95, with any shortfall recorded, because for a screening aid a missed tumor is the more costly error than an unnecessary referral. High accuracy is also not by itself evidence of clinical usefulness, since presenting an accuracy figure as proof of efficiency conflates two different claims ([Chen et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11608314/)).

### 2.1.8 Dataset partitioning and evaluation validity

How a dataset is divided determines whether a reported figure means what it appears to mean. The standard division is into training, validation, and test portions, with the test set withheld from training and from threshold tuning so that the final metrics are computed once on data the model has not seen ([Hicks et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8993826/)). Studies of brain tumor classification do not always follow that discipline: a review of lightweight models reports that earlier work introduced data leakage by evaluating on test sets that contained augmented copies of training images, and by using splits that were not standardized ([Kantu et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13103188/)). Cross-validation is the alternative validation strategy used where data are scarce, and the six-architecture transfer learning comparison already discussed reported 99.96% accuracy under it, while noting that its smaller-dataset figures were obtained without it ([Rasa et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11459499/)).

Two risks follow from this literature, and both bear on how a single accuracy figure should be read. The first is leakage between partitions, which can arise when augmented copies of the same image fall on both sides of a split, or when related images are divided across partitions without a check ([Kantu et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13103188/)). The second is comparability: a review of 83 CNN studies of brain tumor classification found that validation methods, performance metrics, and training data are reported inconsistently enough that no methodological choice can be shown to clearly outperform the alternatives ([Xie et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9406354/)). A headline accuracy therefore has to be accompanied by the protocol that produced it, including the partitioning rule, the handling of duplicates, and the extent to which augmentation was confined to training data ([Xie et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9406354/); [Kantu et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13103188/)). Patient-level separation, the usual remedy when several images come from one person, is not available for the public slice-level datasets used here, which carry no patient identifiers; the study relies instead on content-based duplicate removal before splitting, on validation and test partitions that receive no augmentation, and on reporting test results per source dataset.

### 2.1.9 Computer-aided screening and clinical decision support

Computer-aided diagnosis is the established term for automated analysis that assists a radiologist rather than replacing one, and it is credited in the review literature with reducing clinician workload and minimizing the risk of human error as MRI demand grows faster than the supply of specialists ([Bouhafra & El Bahi, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12092918/)). The setting shapes how such tools are used: where equipment and specialist expertise are scarce, as documented for low- and middle-income countries, the literature points to better exploitation of information technology and artificial intelligence as part of the response ([Frija et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8318869/)), and lightweight designs aimed at exactly that setting have been proposed for detection on edge devices ([Abdusalomov et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11759171/)).

Whether such a tool helps in practice depends on how its output fits a clinician's workflow. A meta-analysis of 36 studies of human–artificial intelligence collaboration in image interpretation found that integrating a model reduced interpretation time by about 27% and, when the model served as a pre-screening step, reduced the number of images requiring human review by about 62%, though the authors caution that the included studies were highly heterogeneous and uneven in quality ([Chen et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11608314/)). The same analysis describes the realistic roles of a model as concurrent aid, second reader, or pre-screening filter — arrangements in which a clinician remains the decision-maker — and observes that deploying artificial intelligence in isolation from human oversight raises ethical and legal concerns ([Chen et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11608314/)).

That is the position this study adopts. NeuroScan is designed as a screening aid that produces a verdict, a confidence value, and an activation heatmap for a clinician or health worker to review; it does not produce a diagnosis and does not act on its own output, and a qualified clinician makes the final decision. The distinction between a model prediction and a clinical diagnosis is therefore stated here as the study's own design position rather than derived from the reviewed literature.

### 2.1.10 Privacy and Local Deployment of Medical Imaging AI

Where inference is performed is a design decision with clinical consequences. Deploying deep learning algorithms through cloud computing raises privacy concerns because patient health information is sensitive and its transfer over a network carries security risks and regulatory barriers ([Dong et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10099365/)). Local deployment avoids that transfer, but bespoke desktop software commonly requires business and security agreements before it is granted filesystem access and must be customized and configured for different devices, which the authors describe as costly at scale ([Dong et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10099365/)). Browser-based local inference was therefore proposed as a middle path that neither copies data away from the workstation nor persists it across sessions, although clinical workstations typically lack graphical processing units, and the demonstrated system required approximately 80 seconds and 1.5 GB of memory to process one volumetric case ([Dong et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10099365/)). The same study notes that simpler clinical tasks such as two-dimensional classification are more tractable on consumer hardware than the volumetric workloads it characterized ([Dong et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10099365/)).

### 2.1.11 Synthesis

Taken together, the literature supports five conclusions that frame the present study. First, CNN-based approaches classify brain MRI with high reported accuracy on public datasets, and lightweight architectures narrow the accuracy gap with substantially fewer parameters, which makes local deployment of a classification model realistic on ordinary hardware ([Kantu et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13103188/); [Abdusalomov et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11759171/)). Second, reported accuracy is a property of the dataset and the protocol as much as of the model: figures spanning 91.63% to 100% appear within a single review, per-class performance can fall well below overall accuracy, and leakage through augmented test sets has been documented in the same literature ([Xie et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9406354/); [Anand et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12868662/); [Kantu et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13103188/)). Third, what is measured shapes what can honestly be claimed: because accuracy alone misleads when the classes are unevenly distributed and no single metric captures every desirable property, screening performance has to be reported as a set of measures obtained on a held-out test set under one documented protocol ([Hicks et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8993826/)). Fourth, class activation mapping is an established way to show which image regions influenced a prediction, and the reviewed studies report that these visualizations localize tumor-relevant areas ([Nazir et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11497403/); [Mohamed Musthafa et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11088067/)). Fifth, two gaps remain in the same body of work: explanation quality, clinician trust, and usability in real settings have rarely been evaluated, and model accuracy is not by itself evidence of clinical usefulness ([Abbas et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12427955/); [Chen et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11608314/)). The present study responds to those gaps by evaluating a trained model once on a held-out test set under one documented protocol, and by delivering its output — verdict, confidence, and activation heatmap — inside a single offline application positioned as a screening aid whose results a clinician reviews.

## 2.2 Related System

This section reviews existing systems that perform CNN-based brain tumor analysis from MRI and deliver the result to a user, organized by how that result reaches the user: web-based diagnosis systems, model-centric classification frameworks without an end-user application, other comparable classification systems, and edge or locally executed deployments. The systems are then compared with one another on task, delivery, explanation, data handling, and validation, and NeuroScan is positioned against them.

### 2.2.1 Web-Based Brain Tumor Diagnosis Systems

The closest comparable system is a web-deployed platform that combines two-dimensional tumor classification with three-dimensional volumetric segmentation ([Aksoy et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12388561/)). Classification used a fine-tuned MobileNetV2 trained on glioma, meningioma, pituitary tumor, and normal-control images, and reached 98.09% accuracy; segmentation used a SegResNet model trained on multi-channel BraTS MRI augmented with synthetic no-tumor data and obtained Dice coefficients of approximately 68% to 70%; a meta-classifier was added for binary tumor detection from volumetric features and reported 100% detection accuracy ([Aksoy et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12388561/)). Explainability was provided through saliency maps for two-dimensional predictions and Gaussian overlays for three-dimensional visualizations, which the authors report were consistent with real pathological features, and the whole pipeline was delivered through a web interface intended for radiology workflows ([Aksoy et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12388561/)).

Three differences from NeuroScan follow directly from the reported design. The classification task is four-class rather than binary, so the system reports tumor type in addition to tumor presence ([Aksoy et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12388561/)). Its segmentation performance is substantially lower than its classification performance, with Dice coefficients of 68% to 70%, which the authors report as tumor segmentations that are usable but not exact ([Aksoy et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12388561/)). And delivery is through a web platform, which presumes the computing and serving environment that accompanies it, whereas NeuroScan is designed to run on the user's own Windows machine with no network access at inference time.

A second web-based system classifies an uploaded scan into four categories — glioma, meningioma, pituitary tumor, and no tumor — through a publicly accessible platform, BTdiagAI ([Ahmmed et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12876050/)). Its classifier fuses three fine-tuned networks (InceptionV3, DenseNet121, and Xception), refines the concatenated features by analysis of variance and L1-based selection, and passes them to a machine learning classifier; five preprocessing methods were compared, all exceeding 98% accuracy, with contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization performing best at 99.8% on one dataset and 99.61% on another ([Ahmmed et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12876050/)). Two differences from NeuroScan matter here. First, the reported output of the platform is a category label, so the user receives a classification rather than a documented explanation of the individual prediction ([Ahmmed et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12876050/)). Second, delivery is through a web service, which requires the image to leave the user's machine and depends on that service being available.

### 2.2.2 Model-Centric Brain Tumor Classification Frameworks

A larger group of studies produces strong models but stops at model-level evaluation. A hybrid framework integrating ResNet101 and Xception in parallel, explained with LIME, reported 99.67% accuracy for tumor detection, with a second pipeline combining a vision transformer, random forest, and adaptive histogram equalization reaching 98.17% ([Vamsidhar et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12216004/)). Comparisons of fine-tuned transfer learning models have likewise reported accuracies from 96.34% to 99.96% depending on dataset size and validation protocol ([Rasa et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11459499/)), and a customized CNN paired with SHAP, LIME, and class activation mapping reached 98.67% validation accuracy with precision, recall, and F1 all reported at 98.50% ([Nazir et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11497403/)). These works report results for the model and its explanation, and their stated scope is model development and evaluation rather than the construction of a screening application, which is consistent with the broader finding that this literature has rarely evaluated usability in real settings ([Abbas et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12427955/)).

### 2.2.3 Other Comparable Brain MRI Classification Systems

Two further studies optimize the classifier itself rather than integrating pre-trained backbones or explanation methods. A four-block convolutional neural network built for the task reached an overall accuracy of 98.5% across glioma, meningioma, pituitary, and no-tumor categories, above the ResNet50 comparison baseline of 93.2%, while its per-class accuracy ranged from 0.86 for glioma to 0.98 for the no-tumor class ([Anand et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12868662/)). Three custom convolutional networks whose hyperparameters were tuned by grid search reported 99.33% for detection, 92.66% for five-class classification across normal, glioma, meningioma, pituitary, and metastatic categories, and 98.14% for three-grade classification, on what the authors describe as large and publicly available clinical datasets ([Irmak, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8061452/)). Together the two results show that a custom network can match transfer learning when the available data are large ([Irmak, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8061452/); [Rasa et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11459499/)), and they also illustrate why cross-study comparisons are fragile: the tasks differ in the number of classes and in difficulty, so figures from the same architecture family are not directly comparable.

### 2.2.4 Edge and Locally Executed Brain Tumor AI Systems

A third group prioritizes where the computation happens. A lightweight RetinaNet variant with a MobileNet backbone was designed for medical edge devices and evaluated on BraTS, reaching an average precision of 32.1 with confidence scores reported above 81%, motivated by the absence of specialized radiological expertise in remote settings ([Abdusalomov et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11759171/)). A browser-based, locally executed deployment for medical imaging AI described the trade-offs of this design space in detail: cloud inference requires transferring sensitive patient information, while bespoke desktop software requires administrative and security approval and per-device configuration, and the proposed alternative ran locally without transferring or persisting data at the cost of roughly 80 seconds and 1.5 GB of memory per volumetric case on machines that typically lack graphics processing units ([Dong et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10099365/)). That characterization is directly relevant to NeuroScan, which accepts the installation and configuration burden of desktop software in exchange for keeping scans on the workstation.

Set side by side, the reviewed systems differ along five dimensions that matter to this study: the task they perform, the way their result reaches a user, whether they explain that result, how their data and validation were handled, and what they leave unvalidated. Four observations follow from the comparison. First, systems that reach an end user are the exception: only two of the studies reviewed here deliver their output through a user-facing platform ([Aksoy et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12388561/); [Ahmmed et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12876050/)), and both are web services that depend on the image being uploaded and on the service remaining available. Second, the tasks are heterogeneous — binary detection, four-class and five-class classification, and volumetric segmentation — so the accuracy figures cannot be ranked against one another, which is the same comparability problem identified at review level ([Xie et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9406354/)). Third, explanations are provided by some systems and not by others, and where they are provided the techniques differ in kind — class activation mapping, saliency maps, and SHAP or LIME ([Nazir et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11497403/); [Aksoy et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12388561/); [Vamsidhar et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12216004/)) — so a heatmap in one system is not interchangeable with a heatmap in another. Fourth, in every case the validation rests on public benchmark datasets or a single internal split rather than on data from another site, so generalizability is unestablished ([Aksoy et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12388561/); [Rasa et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11459499/); [Anand et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12868662/); [Ahmmed et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12876050/)), and the review literature finds that explanation quality, clinician trust, and usability in real settings have rarely been evaluated ([Abbas et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12427955/)) — a limitation this study inherits rather than resolves. Within the set compared in Table 2-1, no system pairs fully local execution with an explanation of the individual prediction and a screening workflow for multiple images.

### 2.2.6 Positioning of NeuroScan

NeuroScan is an offline Windows desktop application that performs binary screening of two-dimensional brain MRI images, classifying each image as tumor present or no tumor and reporting a confidence score together with a class activation mapping (CAM) heatmap that indicates the image regions associated with the decision. The trained convolutional neural network is exported and embedded in the application, so preprocessing, classification, and heatmap generation all occur locally on the user's computer without transmitting scans to external servers, and the application requires no network connection during screening. Beyond single-image screening, NeuroScan provides batch folder screening with live progress reporting, a screening history that persists across application restarts, and export of results as a CSV summary and as heatmap-annotated image copies. It is positioned as an assistive triage aid for research and educational use, not as a medical device, and a disclaimer stating that a qualified clinician makes final decisions is presented in the application.

Positioned against the systems reviewed above, NeuroScan differs in four respects. Where the closest comparable system performs four-class classification and volumetric segmentation through a web platform ([Aksoy et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12388561/)), NeuroScan narrows the task deliberately to binary tumor-versus-no-tumor screening and delivers the result through a self-contained desktop application. Where the two web-based systems reviewed here require the scan to be uploaded to a service ([Aksoy et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12388561/); [Ahmmed et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12876050/)), NeuroScan performs preprocessing, classification, and heatmap generation on the machine that holds the image, with no network access at screening time. Where most reviewed works report model and explanation metrics without an accompanying end-user application, NeuroScan wraps the model in a screening workflow that includes batch processing, persistent history, and exportable results — the operational features that a screening routine requires. And where the deployment literature identifies administrative approval and per-device configuration as the cost of local desktop software ([Dong et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10099365/)), NeuroScan accepts that cost in return for keeping patient images entirely off the network. Its scope is correspondingly narrower than segmentation-based systems: it localizes the model's attention rather than the tumor boundary, and it does not identify tumor type, grade, or stage. It also does not claim what the reviewed literature leaves unevaluated — that a heatmap is useful to the person who reads it ([Abbas et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12427955/)) — nor does it treat a screening verdict as a diagnosis.

## 2.3 Concept of the Study

The conceptual framework of this study is a single input–process–output model that traces how a brain MRI image becomes a screening result inside the NeuroScan desktop application. The framework is presented as Figure 2-1, and the paragraphs below describe each of its components in turn. The framework itself is the study's own synthesis; the constructs it contains are grounded in the sources cited for each component.

### 2.3.1 Input

The primary input is a two-dimensional brain MRI image, the modality that provides superior contrast of brain anatomical regions compared with computed tomography and is therefore the preferred method for identifying brain tumor malignancy ([Bouhafra & El Bahi, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12092918/)). Images of this kind are drawn from publicly available labeled brain MRI datasets of the type used throughout the reviewed classification literature ([Nazir et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11497403/); [Kantu et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13103188/)). The second input is the trained convolutional neural network itself, obtained by adapting a pre-trained backbone through transfer learning, an approach adopted because it provides better accuracy even when the number of available training samples is small ([Rasa et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11459499/)). The third element is the intended user and setting, and it enters the framework as a design constraint rather than as an input to the model. The framework assumes a clinician or health worker working where specialist radiological expertise or advanced computing infrastructure may not be available, which is the situation that motivates lightweight and locally deployable screening tools ([Abdusalomov et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11759171/); [Frija et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8318869/)). It is not supplied to the network: no user attribute is an input to the convolutional neural network, and the model's only input remains the MRI image. What the setting determines is the surrounding design — a lightweight architecture suited to machines that typically lack graphical processing units ([Kantu et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13103188/); [Dong et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10099365/)), screening that works without a network connection, and a clinician review step placed after the result and outside the application's boundary. The user sees only the output — verdict, confidence, and heatmap — and decides what to do with it.

### 2.3.2 Process

The process component begins with preprocessing, which standardizes every image to the input format expected by the model; during training, the same pipeline is supplemented with augmentation to enlarge the image pool and reduce overfitting ([Rasa et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11459499/)). Classification is then performed by the convolutional neural network, which learns discriminative image features directly from image data rather than relying on hand-crafted features ([Vamsidhar et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12216004/)). The network returns a binary verdict — tumor present or no tumor — together with the probability it assigns to each class, and a class activation mapping step then converts the network's internal feature activity into a heatmap highlighting the regions that contribute most to the predicted class ([Nazir et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11497403/)). Both steps execute inside the desktop application on the user's own computer rather than on a remote server, because processing in the cloud requires sensitive patient information to cross the network and thereby introduces security risks and regulatory barriers ([Dong et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10099365/)). A lightweight architecture is used for this component so that inference remains practical on ordinary consumer hardware, since heavier architectures are less suitable for low-resource or real-time clinical environments ([Kantu et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13103188/)).

### 2.3.3 Output

The output component is the screening result presented to the user: a tumor or no-tumor verdict, a confidence value, and the activation heatmap overlaid on the source image. The framework treats these as decision-support information rather than as a diagnosis, consistent with the position that transparency in an automated decision is essential for the clinician who must act on it and that models may otherwise attend to non-discriminative or irrelevant tissue ([Iftikhar et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12044100/)). The output component also carries the operational artifacts of a screening routine — batch results, a persistent screening history, and exported CSV and annotated image files — which are provisions of the study's own design rather than claims drawn from the literature. Because explanation fidelity, clinician trust, and usability in real-world settings have rarely been evaluated in this field ([Abbas et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12427955/)), the framework presents the heatmap as an aid to inspection and makes no assumption that a user will accept it.

### 2.3.4 Boundaries of the Framework

Three assumptions delimit the framework and are stated here as the study's own design choices rather than as findings drawn from the reviewed literature. First, the framework treats a single two-dimensional MRI slice as one screening unit, so volumetric series and cross-slice reasoning are outside it. Second, the framework assumes a binary decision, so tumor type, grade, and stage lie outside it even where the model retains finer labels for future work. Third, the framework assumes that a qualified clinician reviews every result, so the application is positioned as an assistive triage aid and not as a diagnostic device.

## 2.4 Definition of Terms

Terms are defined in two ways. The constitutive definitions below state what a term means as established in the cited literature. The operational definitions, marked as such, state how the term is used within this study and are the study's own definitions. Only terms the study actually uses are defined: the metric terms below correspond to the evaluation plan described in §2.1.7 and §2.1.8, and techniques or instruments that appear in the literature but play no part in this study are not listed here.

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Brain tumor | Abnormal and uncontrolled multiplication of cells within the brain, potentially developing in any part of the brain, skull, or its covering, with symptoms that vary according to the affected region ([Bouhafra & El Bahi, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12092918/)). |
| Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) | An imaging modality that provides superior contrast of brain anatomical regions compared with computed tomography and is the most common technique for detecting and diagnosing brain tumors ([Bouhafra & El Bahi, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12092918/); [Aksoy et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12388561/)). |
| Computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) | An automated system that assists radiologists in decision-making, reducing their workload and minimizing the risk of human error ([Bouhafra & El Bahi, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12092918/)). |
| Convolutional neural network (CNN) | A deep neural network that learns discriminative image features directly from image data rather than from hand-crafted features ([Vamsidhar et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12216004/)); such networks learn automatically, recognize complex patterns and shapes, and have self-learning properties ([Rasa et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11459499/)). |
| Transfer learning | The reuse of knowledge acquired by a model pre-trained on one task to improve performance on another task, which can provide better accuracy even when the number of training samples is small ([Rasa et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11459499/)). |
| Data augmentation | The enlargement of a training set by generating additional images, applied in the reviewed literature to reduce overfitting ([Rasa et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11459499/)) and to address class imbalance ([Khan et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12149374/)). |
| Class imbalance | Unequal representation of the classes in an evaluation dataset; accuracy is known to be misleading when class proportions differ, and thresholds tuned on imbalanced data should be examined for bias toward either class ([Hicks et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8993826/)). |
| Binary classification | A classification problem in which each sample is assigned to one of two classes, designated positive and negative ([Hicks et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8993826/)). |
| Training, validation, and test sets | The three phases of model development, in which the model is trained on a training set, validated on data that is not part of training, and tested on a test set for which the final metrics are calculated, the test data having been set aside after training and tuning are finished ([Hicks et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8993826/)). |
| Confusion matrix | The four quantities produced by a binary classifier — true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives — from which the common performance metrics are calculated ([Hicks et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8993826/)). |
| Accuracy | The ratio between correctly classified samples and the total number of samples in the evaluation dataset, bounded to [0, 1] and known to be misleading when the class proportions differ ([Hicks et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8993826/)). |
| Precision | The ratio between correctly classified samples and all samples assigned to that class, the positive case of which is termed the positive predictive value ([Hicks et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8993826/)). |
| Recall (sensitivity) | The rate of positive samples correctly classified, also known as the sensitivity or true positive rate ([Hicks et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8993826/)); clinically, the proportion of true positive results among all patients who have the condition ([Shreffler & Huecker, 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK557491/)). |
| Specificity | The negative-class counterpart of recall, being the rate of negative samples correctly classified ([Hicks et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8993826/)); clinically, the percentage of true negatives among all subjects who do not have the condition ([Shreffler & Huecker, 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK557491/)). |
| F1 score | The harmonic mean of precision and recall, which penalizes extreme values of either measure and is not symmetric between the two classes ([Hicks et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8993826/)). |
| Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and area under the curve (AUC) | A method for summarizing the performance of a test with continuous outputs across all possible cutoff values, where the AUC reflects the test's ability to distinguish between diseased and non-diseased individuals, ranging from 0.5 (no better than chance) to 1.0 (perfect discrimination) ([Çorbacıoğlu & Aksel, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10664195/)). |
| Youden index | A summary index used to identify the optimal cutoff value of a test, where the optimal cutoff is the value that maximizes the test's sensitivity and specificity ([Çorbacıoğlu & Aksel, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10664195/)). |
| Screening threshold (τ) *(operational)* | The probability at or above which an image is labelled tumor; selected on the validation set by maximizing the Youden index subject to a sensitivity of at least 0.95, and stored with every result so that a verdict remains traceable to the operating point that produced it. |
| Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) | The techniques used to analyze and interpret how complex models arrive at their decisions, providing an understanding of how deep learning models reach their predictions and thereby enhancing transparency and accountability ([Iftikhar et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12044100/)). |
| Class activation mapping (CAM) | A visualization technique that generates a heatmap highlighting the regions of an input image that contribute the most to a specific class prediction ([Nazir et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11497403/)). |
| Patient health information (PHI) | Patient-identifiable medical data whose sensitivity limits network transfer and the installation of software requiring offsite computation, raising security risks and regulatory barriers ([Dong et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10099365/)). |
| ONNX (Open Neural Network Exchange) | An open format for representing machine learning models that defines a common set of operators and a common file format, enabling a model to be used across a variety of frameworks, tools, runtimes, and compilers ([ONNX, n.d.](https://onnx.ai/)). |
| Screening *(operational)* | In this study, the classification of a single brain MRI image as tumor or no tumor to support triage; it is not a diagnosis. |
| Verdict *(operational)* | The binary label displayed for an image, either tumor or no tumor, derived from the model's probability for the positive class. |
| Confidence score *(operational)* | The model's estimated probability for the predicted class, reported as a value between 0 and 1 alongside the verdict. |
| Heatmap overlay *(operational)* | The class activation map rendered over the source MRI image so that the image regions associated with the decision can be visually inspected. |
| Batch folder screening *(operational)* | The processing of all supported images in a user-selected folder in a single operation, with live progress reporting and a results grid. |
| Screening history *(operational)* | The locally stored record of previous screenings, retained across application restarts so that earlier results can be reviewed or exported. |
| Offline inference *(operational)* | Model execution performed entirely on the user's computer with no network access at screening time, so that no image leaves the machine. |
| Held-out test set *(operational)* | In this study, the portion of the dataset withheld from training and from threshold selection, used once to report the final performance measures. |
