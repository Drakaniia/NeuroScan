## Background of the Study

The human brain is the body's most complex organ, and it governs the majority of the functions on which life depends ([Missaoui et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12074157/)). Clinical diagnosis is a vital component of modern healthcare, particularly for disorders such as brain cancer, which ranks among the deadliest in the world ([Anand et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12868662/)). Brain and central nervous system (CNS) cancers caused 321,624 new cases and 248,403 deaths worldwide in 2022 ([Kim et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11060-025-05164-0)). Malignant brain tumors in the United States carry a five-year relative survival rate of only 34.8% ([CBTRUS, 2025](https://cbtrus.org/cbtrus-fact-sheet/)). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is widely used to evaluate brain tumors because it provides detailed images without ionizing radiation ([Bouhafra & El Bahi, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12092918/)). However, conventional MRI may not reliably distinguish brain tumors from non-neoplastic lesions or differentiate some tumor types ([Co et al., 2025](https://www.cureus.com/articles/350114-utility-of-magnetic-resonance-spectroscopy-and-perfusion-imaging-in-differentiating-brain-tumors-from-mimics-in-a-tertiary-hospital-in-the-philippines)). In a retrospective review of 196 pediatric brain tumor patients at the Philippine General Hospital between 2015 and 2019, the median prediagnostic symptomatic interval was 80.5 days ([Orduña & Lubaton-Sacro, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9280403/)). Longer diagnostic intervals were associated with more physician consultations before subspecialist referral and with longer delays between the request for neuroimaging and its facilitation ([Orduña & Lubaton-Sacro, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9280403/)). These results emphasize the significance of improving prompt and accurate brain tumor evaluation in the Philippine setting ([Orduña & Lubaton-Sacro, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9280403/)).

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have shown excellent results in brain MRI analysis, with one recent four-block CNN reporting an overall accuracy of 98.5% on a four-class task covering glioma, meningioma, pituitary, and no-tumor images ([Anand et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12868662/)). Such networks are effective because they learn discriminative features directly from image data rather than from hand-crafted features ([Vamsidhar et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12216004/)). Recent studies and reviews have shown that machine learning and deep learning techniques can support brain tumor detection, segmentation, and classification with high accuracy ([Missaoui et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12074157/)). Achieving high predictive performance is not the only consideration when selecting a model, however, since computationally intensive architectures are less practical for deployment in low-resource or real-time clinical environments ([Kantu et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13103188/)). The increasing complexity of deep learning models may also make their predictions difficult to interpret, which could limit their applicability in medical settings ([Iftikhar et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12044100/)). A further consideration is privacy, since brain MRI scans comprise sensitive patient data and violations have been documented in the scanning, storing, transferring, analyzing, and sharing of such images ([Kapkiç & Sağıroğlu, 2023](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/ijiss/article/1212964)); analysis performed in the cloud requires that information to cross a network, which raises privacy concerns together with security and regulatory barriers ([Dong et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10099365/)). Selecting a CNN model for brain MRI classification therefore involves a trade-off among classification accuracy, computational efficiency, deployment requirements, and interpretability, because increased architectural complexity raises computational cost and impedes real-time implementation ([Iftikhar et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12044100/)) while incomplete transparency limits adoption in clinical practice ([Abbas et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12427955/)).

Transfer learning allows a pre-trained convolutional neural network to be adapted to a new classification task, and it can provide better accuracy even when the number of available training samples is small ([Rasa et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11459499/)). A recent study fine-tuned three pre-trained architectures to distinguish glioma, meningioma, and pituitary tumors together with normal brain MRI scans across 4,517 images, and its best model reached 99.2% accuracy ([Khan et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12149374/)). Another framework integrated two pre-trained models, ResNet101 and Xception, in parallel and applied local interpretable model-agnostic explanations to the result, reporting 99.67% accuracy for tumor detection ([Vamsidhar et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12216004/)). Studies using lightweight architectures report strong performance with far fewer parameters, reaching 99.31% test accuracy with 3.47 million parameters on a 7,023-image dataset ([Kantu et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13103188/)). A systematic review of 83 CNN studies published between 2015 and 2022 found that the approach has been widely applied to brain tumor classification from MR images, with reported accuracy ranging from 91.63% to 100% for distinguishing meningioma, glioma, and pituitary tumors ([Xie et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9406354/)).

Existing studies have shown that CNN-based models can effectively classify brain MRI images and that explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) methods can provide visual insights into model predictions ([Iftikhar et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12044100/)). However, this work remains largely organized around the model itself, since a meta-analysis of explainable clinical decision support research identified an absence of studies evaluating explanation fidelity, clinician trust, or usability in real-world settings ([Abbas et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12427955/)), a systematic review of CNN brain tumor classification studies likewise found that few such studies had focused on clinical usability ([Xie et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9406354/)), and among the systems reviewed for this study only one reports delivering its model through a user-facing platform, a web interface intended for radiology workflows ([Aksoy et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12388561/)). These limitations are particularly relevant in low-resource settings, where shortages of imaging equipment and of radiologists are documented in low- and middle-income countries ([Frija et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8318869/)) and where a review of Philippine General Hospital pediatric cases concluded that better neuroimaging access may lead to earlier diagnosis ([Orduña & Lubaton-Sacro, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9280403/)). Addressing this gap requires a lightweight, offline screening application capable of classifying brain tumor images while presenting accessible visual explanations and comprehensive results through a user-oriented interface.

This study addresses this need through the development of **NeuroScan**, an offline Windows desktop application that integrates CNN-based brain MRI classification with class activation mapping (CAM)-based visualization to provide a practical and interpretable screening tool for resource-constrained healthcare settings. The application is designed to operate entirely on the user's computer, with the trained model embedded within the desktop application, enabling image preprocessing, classification, and CAM visualization without requiring cloud-based services or transmitting MRI scans to external servers.

## Objectives of the Study

### General Objective

The general objective of this study is to develop and evaluate NeuroScan, an offline Windows desktop application that performs binary screening of 2D brain MRI images (tumor present vs. no tumor) using a convolutional neural network (CNN) and provides a confidence score and a CAM visual explanation to support clinical triage without replacing professional diagnosis.

### Specific Objectives

This study specifically seeks to:

1. To design and train a CNN model for binary classification of 2D brain MRI images (tumor vs. no tumor) using two publicly available Kaggle datasets — Br35H and the Brain Tumor MRI dataset — combined into a single working set.
2. To develop and integrate the trained CNN model into a Windows-based desktop application that supports single-image and batch-folder MRI screening with live progress reporting.
3. To integrate a class activation mapping (CAM) visual explanation, computed from the model's classification-head weights, that identifies image regions associated with the model's classification output.
4. To evaluate the trained model using a held-out test set and report performance measures including accuracy, precision, recall (sensitivity), specificity, F1-score, ROC-AUC, and a confusion matrix.
5. To assess the functional suitability of the NeuroScan prototype through test cases that verify core features such as image loading, screening, heatmap generation, history browsing, and result export.

## Significance of the Study

> **Note:** The NeuroScan system is intended as a research prototype and decision-support aid, not as a diagnostic tool or a replacement for professional medical judgment.

The study may benefit the following:

- **Healthcare Practitioners (e.g., radiologists and clinicians)** — NeuroScan may potentially provide decision-support information by generating tumor/no-tumor predictions, confidence scores, and CAM heatmaps.
- **Hospitals and Diagnostic Centers** — The proposed system may provide features that could support local, offline image-screening workflows, such as batch processing, session history, and export functions.
- **IT and Healthcare Technology Developers** — The study may serve as a reference for developing privacy-conscious, locally deployed medical AI applications.
- **Researchers** — The work may serve as a reference for future studies on explainable and lightweight medical image classification systems.
- **Students and the Academic Community** — The project demonstrates the integration of AI, computer vision, explainable AI (XAI), and desktop application development within an IT capstone context.
- **Patients** — The proposed system may potentially support preliminary image screening and assist in identifying cases that may warrant further clinical evaluation.

## Scope and Delimitations

This study focuses on the development and evaluation of NeuroScan, an offline Windows desktop application for binary screening of 2D brain MRI images. The system uses a convolutional neural network (CNN) to classify each image as "tumor" or "no tumor" and provides a confidence score and a CAM heatmap to support clinical triage. The model is trained and evaluated using two publicly available Kaggle datasets of 2D brain MRI images — Br35H and the Brain Tumor MRI dataset — which are merged into one binary working set after exact duplicates are removed. Model performance is assessed on a held-out test set that is not used during training. The application supports single-image and batch-folder screening, maintains a browsable screening history, and allows users to export results as CSV summaries and heatmap-annotated image copies. All image processing and inference are performed locally on the user's machine without uploading scans to external servers.

The study is limited only to 2D MRI images of the brain and excludes other imaging modalities such as CT, PET, and ultrasound. The model performs binary classification only (tumor vs. no tumor) and does not identify specific tumor types, grades, or disease stages. It does not segment tumors, determine exact tumor boundaries, measure tumor size, or predict treatment response or patient outcomes. NeuroScan is intended as a screening and triage aid only and does not provide a diagnosis or replace the judgment of radiologists, physicians, or other qualified healthcare professionals. The application runs on Windows desktop computers only; web, mobile, cloud-based, multi-user, and hospital-information-system integrations are outside the scope of the study. The system uses publicly available datasets rather than clinical data from specific hospitals, and its results may not generalize to all scanners, institutions, or patient populations. The generated CAM heatmaps are explanatory visualizations and are not intended as definitive clinical evidence.
